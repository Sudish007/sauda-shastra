## Emerging markets are an index-provider classification

“Emerging” does not mean small, poor or early-stage. It is an index-provider classification built from market accessibility, foreign-ownership rules, custody, trading and economic development. A country can be technologically advanced and still sit in an emerging-market index. Different providers can classify the same country differently.

{{label:EVIDENCE}} The [MSCI Emerging Markets factsheet](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/c0db0a48-01f2-4ba9-ad01-226fd5678111), dated {{date:2026-09-30}}, put Taiwan at 28.94%, South Korea at 21.40%, China at 19.80%, India at {{fact:glb-em-india-weight}} and Brazil at 4.09%. The first three markets together made up more than two-thirds of the index.

That composition makes the name less useful than the weights. A broad EM holding is heavily exposed to semiconductor supply chains, Korean conglomerates, Chinese policy and internet businesses, then India and a long tail of countries. Its result may have little connection to a generic story about young populations.

Country weights also change with prices, free-float rules, new listings and accessibility decisions. Record the factsheet date whenever a concentration claim is made.

## What an Indian investor really adds

An Indian household already has domestic salary, property, taxes, pension and often a large India equity holding. Buying an EM index adds only the index’s India weight to that exposure. Most of the allocation goes elsewhere, especially North-East Asia.

:::example Look through the headline
Put ₹10,00,000 into a hypothetical fund that tracks the dated MSCI EM weights exactly. India exposure is about ₹1,06,500 because India is {{fact:glb-em-india-weight}} of the index. Taiwan exposure is about ₹2,89,400 from the factsheet’s 28.94% weight; South Korea is about ₹2,14,000 and China about ₹1,98,000.

The example ignores fees, cash, tracking difference and weight changes. It shows that an EM fund is not a second India fund: roughly ₹8,93,500 of this dated look-through sits outside India.
:::

{{label:MECHANISM}} Whether that is useful depends on the existing foreign allocation. An investor with only India gains genuine geographic and currency breadth. An investor who already owns a global fund may simply add more of countries already held. Compare look-through country and sector weights, not fund names.

## Historical return is not the forward case

The same [MSCI factsheet](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/c0db0a48-01f2-4ba9-ad01-226fd5678111) reported a net US-dollar return of 29.22% over the latest year, 9.09% a year over ten years and 9.11% a year since the end of 2000. Net index returns use MSCI’s withholding convention, not an Indian investor’s exact fund tax or cost.

{{label:EVIDENCE}} [AQR’s 2026 capital-market assumptions](https://www.aqr.com/-/media/AQR/Documents/Alternative-Thinking/AQR-Alternative-Thinking---2026-Capital-Market-Assumptions.pdf?sc_lang=en) put the year-end 2025 MSCI EM CAPE at 23, around the ninety-eighth percentile of its history since 2001. Its model estimated 4.8% local real return for EM excluding China and 5.8% for China. High percentile and positive expected return can coexist because the model also uses growth, payout and normalisation assumptions.

These figures reject two shortcuts. Emerging markets are not automatically cheap, and a lower multiple than the US is not a timing signal. The [CAPE guide](../cape-expected-returns-and-currency/) shows how to turn valuation into a range rather than a call.

## Active management has a hurdle too

The case for active EM management is intuitive: governance, state ownership, foreign-access limits and uneven disclosure may create opportunities for research. The cost and evidence still need checking.

{{label:EVIDENCE}} The [SPIVA U.S. Year-End 2025 scorecard](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/spiva/article/spiva-us-year-end-2025/) reported that 53% of US-domiciled emerging-market funds underperformed the S&P Emerging Plus benchmark during 2025. That is one year, one domicile and one benchmark. It cannot answer whether an India-domiciled fund will outperform after Indian tax.

A fair comparison uses the fund’s actual benchmark, total return, identical dates and survivorship-aware category data. It also asks whether the current manager and process produced the record. A concentrated China or technology underweight can explain a result that looks like security selection.

## Political and currency risk are part of the asset

{{label:MECHANISM}} EM risk is not one volatility number. It includes:

- capital controls and foreign-ownership limits;
- state intervention, sanctions and changes in listing access;
- weaker minority-shareholder protection in some markets;
- commodity dependence in exporters;
- dollar funding stress and local-currency depreciation;
- index concentration in a few countries, sectors and companies;
- custody, settlement and tax rules that differ by market.

Currency works at several layers. A Taiwanese company may earn dollars, its shares trade in local currency, the index may report in dollars and the Indian holder files in rupees. A weak local currency can help an exporter’s operations while reducing the translated share return. A dollar share class does not erase that chain.

Diversification helps with one-country shocks but cannot remove a global dollar squeeze. In a risk-off episode, several EM currencies and equities can fall together while the rupee also moves.

## Pick the route and benchmark deliberately

An Indian resident has four broad choices:

1. **A global fund.** EM appears at its global market weight; no separate regional decision.
2. **A broad EM fund.** One allocation covers the full provider universe and its current concentrations.
3. **A regional or single-country fund.** A much narrower active thesis, with higher governance and timing risk.
4. **An India-domiciled international fund.** Rupee dealing and domestic statements, subject to scheme availability and the industry cap {{fact:glb-mf-overseas-cap}}.

An overseas broker such as [Interactive Brokers India](https://www.interactivebrokers.co.in/en/accounts/individual-account-steps-in.php) can provide access to foreign-listed ETFs for eligible resident accounts, without overseas leverage or derivatives. UCITS and US-listed funds can track similar indices while creating different estate-tax and withholding treatment. [Investing abroad from India](../investing-abroad-from-india/) maps those legal differences.

Foreign-listed holdings become long-term for Indian tax only after {{fact:glb-foreign-stock-ltcg-period}} and then use {{fact:glb-foreign-stock-ltcg-rate}} without indexation. A shorter holding is taxed at the slab. Rebalancing a volatile regional tilt can therefore realise tax more often than holding a global core.

## An EM allocation rule

- State whether the purpose is geographic diversification, valuation or a specific country thesis.
- Compare country and sector look-through with every existing global and India fund.
- Use the same index provider and date when comparing weights or returns.
- Separate back-filled index history, live index history and live fund tracking.
- Inspect fund domicile, withholding convention, replication method, spread, assets and securities lending.
- Cap a dedicated regional tilt before a rally or crisis tests discipline.
- Rebalance by calendar or band, not a political headline.

:::evidence What the evidence says
- {{label:EVIDENCE}} MSCI supplies the dated composition and return record; AQR supplies a disclosed valuation model; SPIVA supplies a US-domiciled active-fund result.
- {{label:MECHANISM}} Look-through overlap, currency layers and governance risks explain what the allocation changes. They do not forecast the next country leader.
- {{label:LORE}} Emerging markets are always cheap or always grow faster in investor returns. Neither follows from GDP growth or the current index label.
