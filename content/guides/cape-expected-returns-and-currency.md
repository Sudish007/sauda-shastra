## What CAPE measures

Ordinary price-to-earnings divides today’s price by one year of earnings. That denominator can be unusually high at the top of a cycle or unusually low in a recession. Shiller CAPE uses average inflation-adjusted earnings over the previous ten years, then divides price by that smoother denominator.

[Robert Shiller’s data page](http://www.econ.yale.edu/~shiller/data.htm) publishes monthly US prices, dividends, earnings, consumer prices and the CAPE series back to the nineteenth century. It is the source to use before reproducing a chart. The linked [Campbell–Shiller 1998 paper](http://www.econ.yale.edu/~shiller/online/jpmalt.pdf) connects high starting valuation with lower long-horizon real returns.

{{label:EVIDENCE}} The relationship is about a distribution over years, not next month. CAPE does not include a timer. Earnings definitions change, sector composition changes and interest rates alter the valuation investors will accept. A high reading can become higher before it mean-reverts; a low reading can accompany a damaged economy.

CAPE is therefore better used as a planning input than a sell signal. It can lower the return assumption, widen the downside case or encourage international diversification without requiring an all-or-nothing market call.

## Turn valuation into a return range

The inverse of CAPE is a cyclically adjusted earnings yield. A CAPE near forty implies an earnings-yield anchor near two and a half per cent. That is not a complete expected return because earnings can grow, dividends can be paid, valuation can change and inflation must be separated from real growth.

A useful decomposition is:

`expected real return ≈ starting payout yield + real earnings growth + annualised valuation change`

The first two terms describe what the companies produce and distribute. The third term admits that an expensive market may stay expensive, become more expensive or de-rate. Write a central case plus a low and high case instead of hiding that uncertainty in one precise percentage.

{{label:EVIDENCE}} [AQR’s 2026 capital-market assumptions](https://www.aqr.com/-/media/AQR/Documents/Alternative-Thinking/AQR-Alternative-Thinking---2026-Capital-Market-Assumptions.pdf?sc_lang=en) put the US CAPE near forty at the start of 2026, around the ninety-sixth percentile of its post-1980 history. Its long-term real-return estimates at the end of 2025 were 3.9% for US large companies, 4.9% for developed markets outside the US and 5.1% for emerging markets. The same report estimated 3.4% real for a global 60/40 mix, below its cited long-run US average.

[Vanguard’s 2026 outlook](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/corporatesite/us/en/corp/vemo/2026-outlook-economic-upside-stock-market-downside.html) reached a similar directional conclusion: muted medium-term US equity returns, with more attractive risk–return trade-offs in high-quality US bonds, US value and non-US developed shares. These are model outputs, not promised returns. Their assumptions should be read, not only their point estimates.

## CAPE is not a country-ranking shortcut

A lower valuation can mean a better starting yield, or it can price slower growth, weaker governance, sector concentration or political risk. Comparing US CAPE with Europe, Japan or emerging markets is useful only after aligning the earnings method, inflation adjustment, index composition and date.

{{label:MECHANISM}} A country decision can be broken into four questions:

1. What cash-flow growth is plausible from today’s margins and reinvestment?
2. What valuation is already paid for that growth?
3. What sector and company concentrations sit inside the index?
4. What currency return reaches the Indian household?

The country guides on [Europe](../investing-in-europe/), [Japan](../investing-in-japan/) and [emerging markets](../investing-in-emerging-markets/) apply that frame. A globally diversified fund avoids making one country forecast, but it still follows market-cap weights and can be concentrated in the largest market.

## Diversification changes concentration, not total risk

The [MSCI ACWI factsheet](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/8d97d244-4685-4200-a24c-3e2942e3adeb) put the United States at {{fact:glb-acwi-us-weight}} of global market capitalisation on {{date:2026-09-30}}. A global-cap portfolio is therefore not an equal-country portfolio; it is a large US holding plus smaller developed and emerging holdings.

[Vanguard’s global-equity research](https://www.vanguardmexico.com/content/dam/intl/americas/documents/mexico/en/global-equity-investing-diversification-sizing.pdf) found that, in the markets it studied, portfolio volatility tended to reach its low point with a substantial international allocation. The range differed by home market. That supports diversification and rejects a universal foreign percentage.

For an Indian resident, home bias is wider than the demat account. Salary, business, property, pension and future spending may all depend on India and the rupee. Foreign equity can diversify those exposures. It also introduces foreign custody, tax, regulation and currency risk. Diversified does not mean simpler.

## USD/INR is part of the return

An Indian investor’s rupee return combines the asset’s dollar return with the currency move:

`(1 + USD asset return) × (1 + USD/INR return) − 1`

The terms multiply; they do not simply add. A weaker rupee raises the rupee value of a dollar asset, while a stronger rupee reduces it. There is no reliable currency premium that can be added to every forecast.

:::example One investment, two reporting currencies
Assume $10,000 is bought when USD/INR is ₹83, so the starting value is ₹8,30,000. The asset later reaches $13,000 while USD/INR is ₹90. The ending value is ₹11,70,000.

The dollar return is 30%. The rupee gain is ₹3,40,000, or roughly 41% of the starting rupees. If the holding qualifies as long-term after {{fact:glb-foreign-stock-ltcg-period}}, the Indian rate is {{fact:glb-foreign-stock-ltcg-rate}}: ₹42,500 before cess and ₹44,200 after {{fact:tax-cess-rate}}. The illustration omits surcharge, fees and foreign tax.
:::

The currency move helped in that path. Reverse ₹83 and ₹90 and it hurts. A spreadsheet should therefore carry at least three currency cases: unchanged, rupee weaker and rupee stronger. The [investing-abroad guide](../investing-abroad-from-india/) adds the remittance and tax mechanics.

## Use expected returns without pretending to know the future

A planning process can be mechanical:

- Start from a low, central and high real return for each asset class, with the source and date.
- Add inflation separately to obtain a nominal case.
- Apply fund expense, tracking difference, withholding, foreign-exchange spread and tax.
- Convert foreign outcomes under several USD/INR paths.
- Test the contribution or withdrawal plan under poor early returns, not only a smooth compound rate.
- Revisit the assumptions annually; do not trade simply because a forecast moved by a fraction.

{{label:MECHANISM}} Expected returns are most useful for deciding savings, diversification and spending. They are weakest as a short-term allocation switch. A household that needs an optimistic return to make a goal work has a savings or goal problem before it has a security-selection problem.

:::evidence What the evidence says
- {{label:EVIDENCE}} Shiller supplies the long CAPE history; Campbell and Shiller document the long-horizon relation; AQR and Vanguard disclose current model assumptions; MSCI supplies the dated global weight.
- {{label:MECHANISM}} Earnings-yield decomposition, scenario ranges and currency multiplication turn those inputs into a plan. They do not time a market.
- {{label:LORE}} A particular CAPE line is an automatic exit, or a weakening rupee permanently boosts foreign returns. Neither claim follows from the cited work.
:::
